What Is a Digital Product Passport? How Scanning a QR Code Changes Everyday Shopping

September 27, 2026
If you have bought a jacket, pair of sneakers, or electronic device recently, you have probably noticed standard QR codes printed on wash tags or box packaging. Soon, those codes will do far more than link to a brand's home page.

Under new European Union regulations, millions of everyday products sold across Europe are receiving a Digital Product Passport (DPP).

To businesses and logistics workers, DPPs require industrial scanners and database integrations. But for everyday consumers, the concept is far simpler: your smartphone is the scanner, and a quick scan reveals the true story behind what you are buying.

Here is what Digital Product Passports mean for consumers, what information they unlock, and how they are changing the way we shop, repair, and recycle.

1. How It Works: Your Smartphone Is the Scanner

You do not need to buy specialized hardware or download heavy third-party apps to access a Digital Product Passport.

Every product equipped with a DPP carries an optical tag—usually a standard QR code or GS1 DataMatrix code—printed on its physical label, care tag, or outer casing.

When you open your smartphone’s camera app and point it at the code:
  1. Your phone reads the unique web link embedded in the tag.
  2. Your browser opens a verified web page linked directly to the product’s official digital file.
  3. The passport displays accurate, regulated data about that specific item.

2. What Information Can You Actually See?

Unlike traditional marketing tags that show glossy promotional ads, the data inside an official DPP is regulated by law. It cuts through "greenwashing" to give shoppers clear, verifiable facts.

When you scan a product passport, you typically unlock five key details:

Verified Sustainability & Carbon Footprint

Instead of vague claims like "sustainably sourced," the passport provides hard data on the item's total carbon footprint, water consumption during manufacturing, and raw material origins.

Real Material Composition

Want to know if a sweater is actually 100% organic cotton or a blend of synthetic polyester? The passport lists every material, organic certification, and whether recycled content was used. It also highlights any hazardous substances or microplastics present.

Repair Guides & Spare Part Ordering

Over 75% of European consumers say they would prefer to repair damaged goods rather than replace them. Scanning a product’s DPP opens official repair manuals, troubleshooting steps, and direct links to order compatible spare parts—helping keep appliances, electronics, and clothing out of landfills.

Proof of Authenticity & Ownership

For high-value goods, luxury fashion, or electronics, the passport acts as a digital certificate of authenticity. It helps protect buyers against counterfeit goods when buying secondhand.

End-of-Life Recycling Instructions

When a product reaches the end of its usable life, scanning the tag shows exact instructions on how to safely dispose of or recycle the item, including where to take old batteries or textiles.

3. When Will You See DPP Codes in Stores?

Digital Product Passports are being rolled out progressively across different industries:
  • Industrial & Electric Vehicle Batteries: Mandatory starting in February 2027.
  • Textiles, Apparel & Footwear: Phasing in throughout 2027.
  • Electronics, Furniture & Appliances: Following between 2027 and 2029.
  •  
By the end of the decade, scanning a product passport before buying will be as commonplace as checking an ingredient list on food packaging.

4. What This Means for Everyday Life

The shift toward Digital Product Passports gives power back to consumers.

Rather than relying on brand advertising, anyone with a smartphone can instantly verify whether a brand lives up to its ethical and environmental promises. It makes fixing broken items simpler, buying secondhand goods safer, and choosing durable products easier than ever before.
